# Decomposition — McKinsey: Structured Problem Solving

- **Source**: [structured-problem-solving-raw.md](./structured-problem-solving-raw.md)
- **Date**: 2026-05-23

## One-line essence
Define the problem as a written sentence, MECE-disaggregate it into a logic tree, label each branch with a falsifiable hypothesis, prune by impact-times-movability, run the cheapest analyses that could kill the live hypothesis, then climb the tree back up into a single answer-first pyramid that a decision-maker can act on.

## Core principles

1. **Structure is the prerequisite for thinking, not the residue of it.** Rasiel's alumnus-distilled motto — "Structure, structure, structure. MECE, MECE, MECE. Hypothesis-driven, hypothesis-driven, hypothesis-driven" — treats those three as the three legs of one stool; Conn says the steps exist precisely "to give you the scope for creativity, which often doesn't exist when your problem solving is muddled." Structure is upstream of math, data, and creativity. (근거: Q9, Q15, Q21)

2. **MECE is a binary test, not a vibe.** Minto's definition — groupings must be "mutually exclusive of each other and collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole" — is operationalized by Rasiel into two yes/no questions per issue list: "Is each one a separate and distinct issue?" and "Does every aspect of the problem come under one (and only one) of these issues?" If either answer is no, the decomposition is broken and you redo it. (근거: Q1, Q10)

3. **Answer first, evidence second — but the answer is a hypothesis, not a verdict.** Minto's writing rule ("give the summarizing idea before you give the individual ideas being summarized") and Rasiel's problem-solving rule ("figure out the solution to the problem before you start [solving it]") are the same rule applied to two different surfaces. Conn enforces it operationally with the "one-day answer / one-hour answer": you must always be able to state a current best answer, and the work plan exists to upgrade its evidence, not to defer it. (근거: Q4, Q12, Q20, Q26)

4. **The pyramid is grounded in the reader's cognitive load, not in style.** Minto: "The mind automatically sorts information into distinctive pyramidal groupings in order to comprehend it" and "a reader … has only a limited amount of mental energy available to him." Ordering supporting ideas top-down isn't preference — it's matching the geometry of comprehension. (근거: Q5)

5. **Prune ruthlessly: impact × ability-to-move.** Conn's pruning rule has two dimensions — "How important is this lever or this branch of the tree in the overall outcome?" and "How much can I move that lever?" Branches that fail either test are dropped from analysis, not analyzed weakly. Rasiel's "we don't boil the ocean" is the same rule expressed as an injunction. (근거: Q13, Q19)

6. **The output is a story for a decision-maker, not a deck of findings.** Conn collapses steps 6 and 7 ("synthesize" and "communicate") because the analysis is not the answer; the answer is the synthesis that lets someone answer "What should I do?". Minto's SCQA introduction (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer) is the on-ramp that makes that story land. (근거: Q7, Q14, Q22, Q27)

7. **Calibrate certainty out loud.** Conn modifies "answer first" with explicit uncertainty: not "This is the answer" but "This is a likely answer under the following sets of conditions." Lead with the answer; surface the premises. (근거: Q23)

## The mental moves (재현 가능한 절차)

1. **Write the problem statement as one sentence, and debate every word.** Sit with the stakeholders. Force a single written sentence. Argue about the action verb, the "or" vs "and", the boundaries. Do not move until everyone agrees the sentence captures why they are in the room. The product is a sentence, not a paragraph. (근거: Q16, Q17, Q25)

2. **Disaggregate the problem into a logic tree — try at least two different cuts.** From the problem statement, branch downward into sub-issues. Limit each node to 2–5 children (three is best). At every node, run the MECE test: distinct? exhaustive? If not, recut. Then do it again with a different decomposition axis — the act of cutting it differently is itself a hypothesis generator. (근거: Q3, Q10, Q11, Q18, Q24)

3. **Label every branch with a falsifiable hypothesis.** Don't leave leaves as topics ("market growth"). Restate each as a claim that could be true or false ("market growth is driven primarily by X segment, which is decelerating"). This converts the tree from a table of contents into a list of bets. (근거: Q9, Q12, Q26)

4. **Prune the tree by impact × movability.** For each leaf hypothesis, ask: (a) how much does it move the outcome we care about, and (b) can we actually change/learn about it? Cross out every leaf that fails either. Do not weakly analyze pruned branches; do not argue further about them. (근거: Q13, Q19)

5. **State a one-hour / one-day answer right now.** Before designing analyses, write down your current best answer to the problem statement, given only what you know in the room. It will be thinly supported — that is the point. This becomes the hypothesis the work plan attacks. (근거: Q20, Q26, Q28)

6. **Build the work plan as evidence that would kill the current answer.** For each surviving leaf, decide what fact, if observed, would confirm or refute it. Start with simple heuristics and explanatory statistics; reach for "big-gun" tools only after the shape of the data is understood. Reject 50-page, three-month plans — they will be obsolete before the first finding lands. (근거: Q20, Q21, Q26)

7. **Iterate: every new fact updates the answer and may recut the tree.** Treat the seven steps as a loop, not a waterfall. Each pass tightens the problem statement, prunes or regrows branches, and revises the day-one answer. (근거: Q28)

8. **Synthesize bottom-up: climb the tree into a pyramid.** Once analyses land, group findings into "same kind of idea" clusters; for each cluster, write the one-sentence summary that the findings collectively imply; for those summaries, write the next-level summary; repeat until you reach a single top sentence. That top sentence is the answer. (근거: Q3, Q22)

9. **Communicate top-down with SCQA + uncertainty.** Open with the SCQA introduction (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer). State the answer first, then the supporting ideas, then their support. Phrase the answer as "This is a likely answer under conditions X, Y" — explicit about which premises it depends on. (근거: Q4, Q7, Q23)

## Heuristics & decision rules

- **MECE test (run at every node):** "Is each child a separate and distinct issue?" + "Does every aspect of the parent fall under exactly one child?" Two yeses or recut. (근거: Q10)
- **Branching cardinality:** "Neither fewer than two nor more than five top-line issues (of course, three is best)." (근거: Q11)
- **Pruning rule:** Keep a branch only if it is both high-impact on the outcome AND something you can actually move/learn about. Fail either ⇒ drop it. (근거: Q19)
- **Answer-availability rule:** If you cannot state a one-hour answer right now, you have not yet defined the problem — go back to step 1. (근거: Q20)
- **Plan-size rule:** "Every time you see a 50-page work plan that stretches out to three months, you know it's wrong." Plans should be short and iterative. (근거: Q20)
- **Analysis order:** Simple heuristics and explanatory stats before heavy/ML tools. Understand shape and scope first. (근거: Q21)
- **Ordering inside a pyramid grouping:** Pick exactly one of four — deductive, chronological, structural, comparative. If none fits, the grouping is wrong. (근거: Q6)
- **Three rules for any pyramid node:** (1) the parent summarizes its children; (2) children at one level are the same kind of idea; (3) children are in a logical order. (근거: Q3)
- **Recommendation phrasing:** "This is a likely answer under the following conditions" — lead with the answer, surface the premises, name the uncertainty. (근거: Q23)
- **Use SCQA for any communication of the answer:** Situation → Complication → Question → Answer; Answer at the bottom of the intro is the top of the pyramid. (근거: Q7)

## Anti-patterns (이 인물들이 거부하는 것)

- **Boiling the ocean** — exhaustive, undirected analysis covering everything in case it matters. Rasiel canonized the phrase and reused it: "It's really hard to boil the ocean, and the ocean's gotten a lot bigger." (근거: Q13)
- **Racing to data-gathering before the problem is defined.** Conn's modal failure: "Teams often race off to data-gathering without really understanding what problem they are trying to solve." Sarrazin: bright people "run off with half of the idea about what the problem is and start collecting data and start building models — only to discover that they've really gone off half-cocked … in the wrong direction." (근거: Q17, Q25)
- **Skipping problem definition ("jump past this step").** Spending hours arguing word choice in the problem statement is *not* over-engineering; not doing it is the most expensive mistake. (근거: Q16, Q17)
- **Asserting answers from past models / pattern-matching to the last engagement** instead of disaggregating the current problem. Failing to try multiple cuts of the tree is what enables this. (근거: Q24)
- **Non-MECE decomposition** — categories that overlap or leave gaps. Adding more categories without testing exhaustiveness is worse than fewer well-chosen ones. (근거: Q1, Q10)
- **Stopping at the analysis** — handing over findings instead of a synthesized story. "They've got their analysis, and they assume that's the answer, and of course it isn't the answer." (근거: Q22)
- **Bottom-up communication / "Background" sections.** Minto: "As a rule of thumb, you never want to have a section labeled 'Background' or 'Introduction.'" A Background section lists subjects rather than ideas, so no argument ever gets made. (근거: Q8)
- **False precision in recommendations** — "This is the answer" with no surfacing of conditions or uncertainty. (근거: Q23)
- **Waterfall plans** — 50-page, three-month work plans that resist iteration. (근거: Q20)
- **Arguing about unmovable branches** — "People spend a lot of time arguing about branches that are either not important or that none of us can change." (근거: Q19)

## When this thinking applies

- **잘 맞는 상황**:
  - Decisions under uncertainty and complexity where consequences are meaningful — Conn's exact framing of "What should I do?" problems. (근거: Q14)
  - Business strategy, policy design, product portfolio choices, organizational redesign — situations where the problem itself is ambiguous and multiple stakeholders see it differently. (근거: Q14, Q17)
  - Any setting where a recommendation must be communicated to a decision-maker who did not do the analysis. (근거: Q22, Q27)
  - Team settings where parallel monologues need to converge into "shoulder to shoulder" work. (근거: Q27)

- **안 맞는 상황** (raw에 명시되어 있진 않지만 raw가 정의하는 범위 밖):
  - Pure execution work where the answer is already known and only doing remains.
  - Creative divergence / brainstorming where the goal is to widen the option space, not narrow it.
  - Problems whose answer is self-evident and where the cost of structuring exceeds the cost of just doing.
  - Very fast feedback loops (minutes, not weeks) where the cost of a one-hour answer ≈ the cost of just trying.

## Open tensions

- **"Hypothesis first" vs. confirmation bias.** Rasiel's "figure out the solution to the problem before you start" and Conn's "one-hour answer" sit uneasily with the risk of motivated reasoning toward that initial guess. Raw's partial resolution: the work plan is explicitly designed to *kill* the live hypothesis (Q26: "support or refute it"), and Conn names availability bias and "sunflower bias" as ones to "always be alert to" (recurring observations). The tension is mitigated by treating the answer as a hypothesis to be falsified, not defended — but raw does not give an explicit protocol for noticing when you've slid from one stance to the other. **Unresolved.**

- **MECE's artificiality vs. real-world messiness.** MECE demands clean partitions; real business problems have overlapping causes and fuzzy boundaries. Raw's partial resolution: Conn's "do two or three different cuts at it, each one giving a bit of a different insight" (Q18) — multiple imperfect MECE decompositions used in parallel substitute for one perfect partition. Still, raw doesn't directly address critics who argue MECE forces false separation. **Partially resolved.**

- **Answer-first vs. uncertainty.** Minto demands the summary up front (Q4); Conn warns against false precision (Q23). Reconciled in raw by Conn explicitly: lead with the answer *and* state the conditions and uncertainty alongside it. The remaining tension is practical — how to do this without burying the lede. **Resolved in principle, open in execution.**

- **Structure as creativity-enabler vs. checklist-rigidity.** Conn anticipates the objection ("isn't this just seven boxes to tick?") and answers that structure absorbs the muddle so creativity has room (Q15). But raw provides no test for when the seven-step process has become performative ritual rather than thinking aid. **Unresolved.**

- **Iteration vs. answer-first commitment.** Conn frames the process as iterative (Q28), with the day-one answer being updated repeatedly. Yet the same Conn warns against "asserting answers from past models" (Q24). The resolution is implicit: iterate the *current* hypothesis against new facts, but don't import yesterday's hypothesis whole-cloth into today's problem. Raw doesn't make this explicit. **Partially resolved.**
