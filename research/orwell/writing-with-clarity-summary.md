# Decomposition — George Orwell: Writing with clarity (clear language as clear/honest thinking)

- **Source**: [writing-with-clarity-raw.md](./writing-with-clarity-raw.md)
- **Date**: 2026-05-24

## One-line essence
Read a foggy sentence as a *symptom*: vague, stale, or inflated language usually means the writer hasn't decided what they mean or is hiding it — so you locate the fog, name the concrete bad habit producing it, force the meaning back into plain concrete words, and check whether the prose now lets the meaning show through rather than just sounding better. (근거: Q3, Q4, Q7, Q14)

## Core principles

1. **Foggy language is a symptom, not just a style nit.** Ugly, inaccurate language and foolish thought are bidirectional: bad thinking produces sloppy language, and sloppy language makes more foolish thinking easier (Q2, Q3). So a vague sentence is evidence to investigate — it is pointing at muddy or undecided thinking underneath — not merely a cosmetic blemish to polish (Q3, Q13). The decay is a set of imitated *habits*, which is also why it is reversible by deliberate effort (Q1, Q12).

2. **The chief enemy of clarity is insincerity.** When there is a gap between your real aim and your declared aim, you reach instinctively for long words and worn-out idioms — "like a cuttlefish spurting out ink" — to cloud the gap (Q4). Vague language is therefore often *doing work*: it conceals that you haven't decided what you mean, or softens something you don't want to say plainly, or dresses emptiness in an "appearance of solidity" (Q4, Q5, Q16). Clarity is honesty made visible.

3. **Good prose is a windowpane.** The goal is transparency: the reader should see the thing, not the writing. You write well not by adding decoration but by effacing yourself so the meaning shows through (Q14). Decorative adjectives, "purple passages," and "sentences without meaning" are precisely what appear when you have nothing definite to say (Q16) — ornament fills the vacuum that meaning should occupy.

4. **Decide what you mean first; the words follow.** Orwell's own retrospective: where he "lacked a political purpose" — i.e., had nothing definite he was trying to expose or assert — he wrote lifeless, meaningless prose (Q16). Writing starts from "some lie I want to expose, some fact I want to draw attention to" (Q15). The first question of the diagnosis ("What am I trying to say?") is therefore prior to all word-level fixes (Q7).

5. **Rules are servants, not masters.** The six rules are tools "to rely on when instinct fails," not a guarantee of good writing — "one could keep all of them and still write bad English" (Q10). The sixth rule explicitly overrides the other five: break any of them rather than say "anything outright barbarous" (Q11-vi). Clarity is the end; the rules are a fallible means to it (Q12).

## The mental moves (재현 가능한 절차)

A 1-turn operation you run on a finished draft. Apply per-sentence (or per-passage) to anything written in ordinary prose — email, report, policy paragraph, essay, abstract.

1. **Adopt the diagnostic stance.** Before fixing anything, treat each foggy, abstract, or boilerplate-sounding sentence as a *symptom* of unclear or insincere thinking underneath — not as a typo to smooth over. Ask: what is this sentence hiding? (근거: Q3, Q4, Q13)

2. **Locate the fog.** Scan for the concrete bad habits and name which one each instance is, because naming tells you how to fix it (근거: Q6):
   - *Dying metaphors* — figures you've seen in print so often they've gone dead ("toe the line," "ride roughshod over").
   - *Operators / verbal false limbs* — phrase-padding that replaces a plain verb and adds syllables for fake symmetry ("render inoperative," "exhibit a tendency to," "make contact with").
   - *Pretentious diction* — words that inflate without adding meaning ("phenomenon," "objective," "categorical," "effective").
   - *Meaningless words* — terms used with no agreed referent ("values," "human," "natural," "vitality").

3. **Run the scrupulous-writer interrogation** on each marked sentence, in order (근거: Q7):
   1. *What am I trying to say?* (If you can't answer, the problem is upstream — you haven't decided your meaning; stop and decide it.)
   2. *What words will express it?*
   3. *What image or idiom will make it clearer?*
   4. *Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?*
   5. *Could I put it more shortly?*
   6. *Have I written anything avoidably ugly?*

4. **Run the honesty check.** For each vague spot, ask which kind of gap it is covering (근거: Q4, Q5, Q16):
   - Have I not actually decided what I mean here? (then: decide, don't decorate)
   - Am I softening or dressing up something I don't want to say plainly? (then: say it plainly, or own that you're choosing not to)
   - Is the abstraction giving "an appearance of solidity to pure wind" — padding emptiness? (then: cut it)
   This is the move that separates a clarity *diagnosis* from a copy-edit: it asks whether plainness would expose something the fog was protecting.

5. **Apply the cut/rewrite moves** driven by the six rules, as operations on the text, not as a mechanical filter (근거: Q11, Q10):
   - Replace each dead/printed metaphor with a fresh image or a literal statement (rule i).
   - Swap each long/Latinate word for the everyday word that fits (rules ii, v).
   - Cut every word that can be cut without loss (rule iii).
   - Prefer the active where it carries the meaning more directly (rule iv) — *but* see Anti-patterns: this is "prefer the more direct construction," not "delete all passives."
   - Override any of the above when obeying it would force something clumsier or falser than the original (rule vi).

6. **Use the before/after test as your model.** Hold the rewrite against Orwell's demonstration: the concrete Ecclesiastes verse ("the race is not to the swift...", Q8) versus its abstract-Latinate parody ("Objective considerations of contemporary phenomena compel the conclusion...", Q9). The parody contains the *same* claim but is dead — built from abstract nouns, pretentious diction, and padding. Ask: did my rewrite move *toward* the concrete original or toward the parody? (근거: Q8, Q9)

7. **Re-read for transparency, not prettiness.** Final pass: can the reader now see the thing through the prose (windowpane), or does the writing still call attention to itself (Q14)? And — separately — did making it plainer actually surface a clearer meaning, or did it just sound cleaner while the thought stayed muddy? (근거: Q14, and see Anti-patterns C8)

## Heuristics & decision rules
- If you can't answer "What am I trying to say?", the defect is in the thinking, not the wording — go decide the meaning before touching the sentence. (근거: Q7, Q16)
- If a phrase reaches for a long word or a worn idiom right where the argument is weakest or most evasive, suspect a gap between real and declared aim, and look there. (근거: Q4, Q5)
- If a word can be cut with no loss of meaning, cut it. (근거: Q11-iii)
- If an everyday word exists, prefer it over the foreign/scientific/jargon word. (근거: Q11-v)
- If a metaphor is one you've often seen in print, treat it as dead and replace it. (근거: Q11-i)
- If obeying a rule would force something "outright barbarous" (clumsier or less true), break the rule — the rule loses to clarity. (근거: Q11-vi, Q10)
- Diagnose by *habit category* (which of the four it is), because the category names the fix. (근거: Q6)

## Anti-patterns (이 인물이 거부하는 것)
- **Treating the six rules as a mechanical linter.** Orwell himself: you can keep all six rules and still write bad English (Q10); rule vi exists precisely to override the others (Q11-vi). The skill must not become "delete every passive / every familiar phrase." (근거: Q10, Q11-vi)
- **A dumb "delete all passives" pass.** Pullum: the anti-passive rule is linguistically confused; most writing-advice claims about the passive are "wildly mistaken," and the people enforcing it often can't even identify a passive (C1, C2). Orwell himself runs ~20% passives in this very essay, above the ~13% he'd find typical (C4). So move iv is "prefer the more direct construction where it carries the meaning," never a blanket ban. (근거: C1, C2, C4)
- **A blanket ban on familiar phrasing.** Pullum calls the "avoid all familiar phrases" demand "insane and unfollowable" — taken literally it forbids nearly all idiom (C3). Kill *dead* metaphors and *padding*, not all conventional language. (근거: C3)
- **Equating "made it plainer" with "made it truer."** Plain language is not automatically honest: spin "aspires to replace awkward complexities with catchy simplicity," and "Let's be clear" + five bogus bullet points is now a *more* common refuge than Latinate diction (C7). A masterly-clear style can sit on top of under-examined logic — Orwell's own essay does (C8). Clarity exposes muddy thinking only sometimes; it can also disguise it. (근거: C7, C8)
- **Treating plain style as neutral/transparent by default.** Freedman-line critique: the "simplistic faith" that plain style is a neutral windowpane ignores that it too encodes particular values (C9). The windowpane is an aspiration, not a guarantee. (근거: C9, C8)
- **Forcing plainness onto genuinely complex ideas.** Distrusting anyone with a broad vocabulary or a complex sentence is a "new puritanism" (C7); some ideas legitimately need difficult language, and forced plainness falsely flattens them. (근거: C7)
- **Adding decoration to compensate for having nothing to say.** Decorative adjectives, purple passages, and meaningless sentences are the *symptom* of an absent purpose, not a cure for weak prose (Q16). (근거: Q16)

## When this thinking applies
- **잘 맞는 상황**: You have already (more or less) decided what you mean, and need to make that meaning land cleanly on the page — and to catch the spots where fog is hiding muddy or evasive thinking. Ordinary prose of any kind: email, report, policy paragraph, product description, essay, abstract. (근거: Q1, Q7, Q14)
- **안 맞는 상황 / boundary against adjacent skills**:
  - Not `ogilvy` — that is persuasive/brand copy meant to *sell*, a multi-turn collaborator; this is making decided meaning transparent, not maximizing persuasion.
  - Not `lee-eo-ryeong-questioning` — that inverts consensus to *coin new meaning*; Orwell's diagnosis works on meaning you already hold and want to express clearly.
  - Not `feynman-explaining-to-understand` — that audits whether you *understand the concept at all*; Orwell assumes you understand it and checks whether the *prose* lets it show (and flags where it doesn't).
  - **Not when the idea is irreducibly complex.** If plainness would distort the content (technical, legal, scientific, poetic registers), the windowpane goal can falsely flatten the idea — apply with judgment, not as a rule. (근거: C7, Gaps)
  - **Not as a truth-meter.** A passage can pass every clarity check and still be logically sloppy or dishonest; "plainer" ≠ "truer." (근거: C7, C8)

## Open tensions
- **Prescription vs. self-limitation, in the same essay.** Orwell issues absolute imperatives (Q11) yet repeatedly undercuts them: rules are only for "when instinct fails," you can obey all and still fail (Q10), break them rather than be barbarous (Q11-vi), and "I have again and again committed the very faults I am protesting against" (C5). *Resolution adopted*: read the rules as a fallible diagnostic aid subordinate to clarity, not as a linter — which is exactly why this skill leads with the diagnostic stance and honesty check, not the rule list. (근거: Q10, Q11, C5)
- **"Clear language ⇒ clear/honest thought" — claimed but not guaranteed.** Orwell ties clear language to clear thinking and even to "political regeneration" (Q3, Q13), but Smith shows clear style can coexist with sloppy logic and serve spin (C7, C8), and Freedman-line critics attack the "simplistic faith" in the language→thought link (C9). *Unresolved*: the skill keeps the diagnostic value (fog often reveals muddy thinking) while explicitly refusing the inference that plainness certifies honesty. (근거: Q3, Q13, C7, C8, C9)
- **Anglophone-flavored rules vs. language-universal moves.** Orwell's specific rules are English-specific (Latinate vs. Anglo-Saxon diction, the English passive, English print-idioms) and the passive rule in particular is contested (C1–C4). But the *underlying moves* — cut clutter, prefer the concrete, decide and say what you mean, don't hide behind jargon, check whether fog covers a gap — are language-universal and apply equally to prose written in Korean. *Resolution adopted*: the procedure above is framed around the moves; the six rules are kept as illustrative tactics, not as the skill's core. (근거: Q4, Q7, Q11, Q14, C1–C4)
- **No sourced rebuttal to the upstream moves.** Critics concentrate on rule (iv) passive and rule (i) familiar-phrase; the raw collected *no* direct rebuttal to "decide your meaning first / cut needless words" (Recurring observations). The diagnostic core therefore rests on less-contested ground than the word-level rules. (근거: Recurring observations, C1–C4)
