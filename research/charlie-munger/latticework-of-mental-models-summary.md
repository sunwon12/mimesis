# Decomposition — Charlie Munger: Latticework of Mental Models

- **Source**: [latticework-of-mental-models-raw.md](./latticework-of-mental-models-raw.md)
- **Date**: 2026-05-24

## One-line essence
Run one problem through the big load-bearing models of several disciplines at once — instead of forcing it onto your home-discipline model — then read where the models agree, conflict, or compound, and only trust the result inside your circle of competence. (근거: Q1, Q2, Q3, Q9, Q10)

## Core principles
1. **Facts are useless until hung on a latticework** — isolated facts you "bang back" are not in a usable form; knowledge only becomes operable when arrayed on a structure of theory/models. Memorizing without the latticework "fails in school and in life." (근거: Q1)
2. **One model tortures reality** — with only one or two models, human psychology makes you bend the problem to fit the model ("torture reality so that it fits your models") rather than the reverse. The single-model operator is "the man with only a hammer" to whom "every problem looks like a nail." This failure mode hits trained experts too, not just amateurs (e.g. mathematically skilled economists assuming away reality to keep using their tool). (근거: Q2, Q11)
3. **The models must cross disciplines** — "all the wisdom of the world is not to be found in one little academic department." A worldly-wise person carries models "across a fair array of disciplines," because each discipline supplies tools the others lack. (근거: Q2, Q3)
4. **A handful of big ideas carry the freight** — you don't need every model; ~80–90 important models cover ~90%, and "only a mere handful really carry very heavy freight." Pull the *big* ideas from the *big* disciplines, not obscure ones. (근거: Q4)
5. **Models have a reliability hierarchy** — weight the more reliable disciplines more heavily: hard science/engineering (backup systems, breakpoints, critical mass) > biology/physiology > psychology (most complex but most important for worldly wisdom). (근거: Q6)
6. **Know each model's limitations** — even an indispensable model (accounting as "the language of practical business life") is "only a crude approximation"; a person working with a cognitive tool, like one working with a physical tool, "has to know its limitations." Knowing the boundary is part of holding the model. (근거: Q5, Q7)
7. **Combined forces produce non-linear extremes (Lollapalooza)** — when several tendencies/factors act "in certain ways" toward the same outcome, the combination's power to change behavior greatly exceeds any one acting alone; in a lollapalooza "you will almost always find four or five of these things working together." (근거: Q7, Q8)
8. **Bound the output by circle of competence** — analyze with the full latticework, but only bet/decide where you have an edge. Everyone has a circle of competence that is "very hard to advance"; play games where you have the aptitude, not where others do. (근거: Q10, C3)

## The mental moves (재현 가능한 절차)
A 1-turn procedure for running a problem/decision through the latticework:

1. **Catch the hammer reaching for the nail.** Before analyzing, notice the *first* model you reflexively reach for — almost always the one from your home discipline or training. Flag it: this is the "man with a hammer" default, and treating it as the whole answer means you'll "torture reality" to fit it. Detecting this default is the trigger for the rest of the procedure. (근거: Q2, Q11)
2. **Pull the big models from the big disciplines and apply each to the same problem.** Deliberately walk other disciplines and apply their load-bearing models to *this one* problem, e.g. math (compound interest, permutations & combinations), engineering (backup system, breakpoints), physics (critical mass), biology/physiology, microeconomics (advantages of scale, competitive destruction), psychology (social proof, Pavlovian association). Don't catalog all 80–90; reach for the handful that carry heavy freight. (근거: Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6)
3. **Run the two tracks.** Analyze the problem on Track 1 — "the factors that really govern the interests involved, rationally considered" (real interests, real probabilities, worked like a bridge problem) — and Track 2 — "the subconscious influences" / psychological tendencies that may be producing wrong conclusions. Hold both at once on the same problem. (근거: Q9)
4. **Cross-check: where do the models agree, and where do they conflict?** Lay the disciplines' verdicts side by side. Disagreement is a signal to keep digging; convergence is the next move. (근거: Q7, Q8)
5. **Read for a Lollapalooza.** When several models/forces point the same direction and *compound*, expect a non-linear, extreme outcome — not the sum of the parts. The detection signal Munger gives: "four or five of these things working together" in the same direction. Weight that case far more heavily than any single-model read suggested. (근거: Q7, Q8)
6. **Weight by model reliability and known limitations.** Where models conflict, lean toward the more reliable disciplines (hard science/engineering over psychology), and discount any model at the edge of its known limitations (accounting as crude approximation; the cognitive apparatus's own limits). (근거: Q5, Q6, Q7)
7. **Bound the conclusion by circle of competence.** Ask whether *this* problem sits inside a domain where you have a real edge. If it's outside — where deep, domain-specific competence you lack is what matters — put it on the "too hard" side and decline, the way Munger/Buffett avoid high-tech "based on our personal inadequacies." The latticework's output is trustworthy only inside this boundary. (근거: Q10, C3)

## Heuristics & decision rules
- If you have only one or two models for a problem, stop — you'll bend reality to fit them; force in more models from other disciplines. (근거: Q2)
- If you catch yourself reaching for your home-discipline tool first, treat that as a warning, not an answer (the "every problem looks like a nail" reflex). (근거: Q2, Q11)
- Don't try to master all models; learn the ~80–90 big ones (~90% of the freight), and prioritize the "mere handful" that carry the heaviest freight. (근거: Q4)
- When models conflict, weight by reliability: hard science/engineering > biology/physiology > psychology. (근거: Q6)
- When 4–5 forces all push the same way, expect a lollapalooza — a non-linear extreme — and weight it accordingly. (근거: Q8)
- For any model, also learn its limitations; an indispensable model can still be "only a crude approximation." (근거: Q5, Q7)
- Run every important problem on two tracks: rational interests/probabilities AND subconscious psychological influences. (근거: Q9)
- Only bet/decide inside your circle of competence; if the edge belongs to someone else's aptitude, you lose — so pass. (근거: Q10, C3)

## Anti-patterns (이 인물이 거부하는 것)
- **Single-model operation (man with a hammer).** Relying on one or two models so that every problem "looks like a nail" and reality gets tortured to fit. Munger names the trained-expert version too: economists good at higher math making unrealistic assumptions just to keep using the tool. (근거: Q2, Q11)
- **Rote facts without the latticework.** Remembering and "banging back" isolated facts that don't hang together — the student who memorizes and fails. (근거: Q1)
- **Single-discipline worldview.** Assuming one academic department holds all the wisdom; Munger's example is the worldly-unwise poetry professor who "doesn't have enough models." (근거: Q3)
- **Chauffeur knowledge / superficial dabbling.** "Learning the talk" without paying the dues — chauffeur knowledge vs. Planck knowledge. The latticework prescription degenerates into this when models are picked up shallowly. Munger himself flags this as a hazard. (근거: C2)
- **Out-of-competence overreach.** Applying the latticework as if it were universally valid in domains where you have no edge; Munger declines high-tech on "personal inadequacies" rather than over-extend the toolkit. (근거: C3, Q10)
- **Parroting models as shallow copies.** Reciting mental models as theory without practice — "knowing something in theory and knowing something in practice aren't the same thing." (근거: C1, C5)

## When this thinking applies
- **잘 맞는 상황**: A single problem/decision that several disciplines plausibly bear on; a low-validity, complex environment where patterns are hard to discern and one discipline alone misleads; situations where multiple forces may be compounding (candidate lollapaloozas); and any decision you can place inside your circle of competence. (근거: Q3, Q7, Q8, Q10, C4)
- **안 맞는 상황**: High-validity domains with clear, repeatable patterns, where a deep domain specialist outperforms a multidisciplinary generalist; domains outside your circle of competence where the relevant edge is expertise you lack; and anywhere the practice is tacit and can't be operated from a model list (knowing-in-theory ≠ knowing-in-practice). (근거: C3, C4, C1, C5, Q10)
- **Boundary against adjacent methods** (for skill disambiguation): This is *multidisciplinary cross-examination of one problem* — pulling many disciplines' big models onto the same target and reading their agreement/conflict/convergence (근거: Q2, Q3, Q7). It is distinct from:
  - **MECE decomposition (McKinsey-style)** — breaking one problem into mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive sub-parts within a structure; the latticework instead overlays multiple *external* lenses onto the whole, undecomposed problem (근거: Q3, Q9).
  - **Causal / first-principles drilling (Aristotle-style)** — drilling *down* into the fundamental causes of one thing; the latticework instead spreads *across* disciplines at the same level (근거: Q3, Q4).
  - **Understanding-self-audit (Feynman-style)** — testing whether you truly understand one concept; the latticework assumes you hold many models and tests how they *combine* on a problem (근거: Q1, Q7, Q8).

## Open tensions
- **Generalist breadth vs. specialist depth.** Munger prescribes multidisciplinary breadth (Q3, Q4), but expertise research holds that in high-validity, pattern-rich domains the deep domain specialist wins and breadth can bring rigidity (C4). Partial resolution: Munger's own circle-of-competence clause concedes specific domains where the latticework gives no edge (Q10, C3), so breadth is bounded rather than universal — but the raw doesn't tell us *how* to decide a domain is high-validity vs. low-validity, so the dividing line is unresolved. (근거: Q3, Q4, Q10, C3, C4)
- **"Know the models" vs. tacit knowledge.** Munger implies arraying 80–90 models makes you worldly-wise (Q1, Q4), while the critique holds that what makes practitioners effective is largely tacit and "nearly impossible to communicate" by model list (C1, C5). Partial resolution: Munger's "hang experience — both vicarious and direct — on the latticework" (Q1) and his chauffeur-vs-Planck distinction (C2) both concede that experience/dues, not the list alone, do the work; unresolved is whether the explicit latticework is the *cause* of skill or merely its scaffold. (근거: Q1, Q4, C1, C2, C5)
- **Lollapalooza word vs. concept location.** The "circumstances combine in certain ways" concept appears in USC 1994 (Q7), but the named "lollapalooza" definition with the "four or five working together" detection signal comes from Harvard 1995 (Q8); the cleanest worked demonstration (the 1996 Coca-Cola talk) is unverified in the raw (see Gaps). The procedure's lollapalooza-reading move (Move 5) leans on Q8 for its signal. (근거: Q7, Q8)
