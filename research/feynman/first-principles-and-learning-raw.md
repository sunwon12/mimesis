## Quotes & evidence

### Q1 — "Atoms in Motion": single sentence as foundation

> "If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge were to be destroyed, and only one sentence passed on to the next generations of creatures, what statement would contain the most information in the fewest words? I believe it is the *atomic hypothesis* (or the atomic fact, or whatever you wish to call it) that all things are made of atoms — little particles that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another. In that one sentence, you will see, there is an enormous amount of information about the world, if just a little imagination and thinking are applied."
>
> — *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. I, Chapter 1 §1-2 "Matter is made of atoms" (1963). <https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_01.html>

**Context**: Opening of the very first lecture Feynman gave to Caltech freshmen. He is justifying why the course will begin by reducing all of physics to one minimal claim from which the rest can be inferred "if just a little imagination and thinking are applied."

### Q2 — "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself"

> "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool. So you have to be very careful about that. After you've not fooled yourself, it's easy not to fool other scientists. You just have to be honest in a conventional way after that."
>
> — Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science," Caltech commencement address, 14 June 1974; *Engineering and Science* 37(7), p. 12. <https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.pdf>; <https://speakola.com/grad/richard-feynman-caltech-1974>

**Context**: Feynman is contrasting genuine science with "cargo cult" practices that mimic scientific forms but lack the underlying epistemic discipline. The "first principle" here is not "first principle" in the philosophical/axiomatic sense (the topic) but is Feynman's own designation for the prerequisite epistemic posture without which all other moves break.

### Q3 — Cargo Cult Science as a diagnosis of fake understanding

> "In the South Seas there is a cargo cult of people. During the war they saw airplanes land with lots of good materials, and they want the same thing to happen now. So they've arranged to imitate things like runways, to put fires along the sides of the runways, to make a wooden hut for a man to sit in, with two wooden pieces on his head like headphones and bars of bamboo sticking out like antennas... They're doing everything right. The form is perfect. It looks exactly the way it looked before. But it doesn't work. No airplanes land."
>
> — Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science," 1974, p. 11.

**Context**: Feynman's central metaphor for activity that follows the surface forms of inquiry (jargon, citations, procedure) without the underlying mechanism that makes it work. The cargo-cult diagnosis is the negative pole of his first-principles posture: if your knowledge would not survive disassembly, you have cargo, not understanding.

### Q4 — Bending over backwards / scientific integrity

> "It's a kind of scientific integrity, a principle of scientific thought that corresponds to a kind of utter honesty — a kind of leaning over backwards... It's a specific, extra type of integrity that is not lying, but bending over backwards to show how you're maybe wrong. Details that could throw doubt on your interpretation must be given, if you know them. You must do the best you can — if you know anything at all wrong, or possibly wrong — to explain it."
>
> — Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science," 1974, pp. 11–12.

**Context**: Feynman's positive prescription: the price of not fooling yourself is the discipline of actively surfacing the most damaging counter-evidence to your own claim. This is the move that converts a Feynman-style explanation from "convincing simplification" into "tested understanding."

### Q5 — The bird's name vs. knowing the thing (father)

> "See that bird? It's a brown-throated thrush, but in Germany it's called a halsenflugel, and in Chinese they call it a chung ling and even if you know all those names for it, you still know nothing about the bird — you only know something about people; what they call that bird. Now that thrush sings, and teaches its young to fly, and flies so many miles away during the summer across the country, and nobody knows how it finds its way..."
>
> — Feynman, "What Is Science?" NSTA 1966 address; reprinted *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out*, pp. 173–. <http://feynman.com/science/what-is-science/>

**Translation (ko)**: "저 새 보이지? 갈색목 개똥지빠귀야. 독일어로는 halsenflügel, 중국어로는 chung ling이라고 부르지. 하지만 그 모든 이름을 다 알아도 너는 새에 대해선 아무것도 모르는 거야 — 다만 사람들이 그 새를 뭐라고 부르는지 알 뿐이지."

**Context**: Feynman's father-told story he uses to define the *operating distinction* between "knowing the name of something" and knowing something. This is the source of the move in Feynman technique that forces description in plain mechanism rather than terminology.

### Q6 — Knowing the name vs. knowing the thing (BBC, 1981)

> "He knew the difference between knowing the name of something and knowing something, which I learned very early."
>
> — Feynman, *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* (BBC Horizon, 1981); transcribed at <https://neverlamentcasually.wordpress.com/2014/02/19/richard-feynman-the-pleasure-of-finding-things-out/>

**Translation (ko)**: "아버지는 어떤 것의 이름을 아는 것과 그것을 아는 것의 차이를 알고 계셨고, 나는 그 차이를 아주 일찍 배웠다."

**Context**: Late-life interview where Feynman traces the *operating distinction* of his learning practice directly to his father. Repetition (1966 → 1981) over 15 years confirms this is not anecdote but a load-bearing principle for him.

### Q7 — Father's habit of testing a guess by looking

> "I guessed it's because the feathers are ruffled, and he's trying to straighten them out. He said, 'Okay, when would the feathers get ruffled, or how would they get ruffled?' 'When he flies. When he walks around, it's okay; but when he flies it ruffles the feathers.' Then he would say, 'You would guess then when the bird just landed he would have to peck more at his feathers than after he has straightened them out... Okay, let's look.' So we would look, and we would watch, and it turned out, as far as I could make out, that the bird pecked about as much and as often no matter how long he was walking on the ground and not just directly after flight. So my guess was wrong, and I couldn't guess the right reason."
>
> — Feynman, "What Is Science?" NSTA 1966 address.

**Context**: A long Feynman set-piece showing the *mental move* his father drilled in: form an explanation, derive a checkable consequence, look, accept disconfirmation. This is Q5/Q6 as a procedure, not a slogan.

### Q8 — Brazil: memorization without understanding

> "I discovered a very strange phenomenon: I could ask a question, which the students would answer immediately. But the next time I would ask the question — the same subject, and the same question, as far as I could tell — they couldn't answer it at all! ... After a lot of investigation, I finally figured out that the students had memorized everything, but they didn't know what anything meant. When they heard 'light that is reflected from a medium with an index,' they didn't know that it meant a material *such as water*."
>
> — Feynman, "O Americano, Outra Vez!" in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985); excerpts at <https://v.cx/2010/04/feynman-brazil-education>, <https://enlightenedidiot.net/random/feynman-on-brazilian-education-system/>

**Context**: Feynman is teaching at the Centro Brasileiro de Pesquisas Físicas (Rio, c.1951). The Brazil chapter is the most extended case study Feynman ever wrote of what fake-understanding looks like in practice — and it is the empirical basis later writers used to extract a "Feynman technique."

### Q9 — Brazil: Brewster's angle and the polaroid at the bay

> "I said, 'Look at the bay outside, through the polaroid. Now turn the polaroid.' — 'Ooh, it's polarized!' they said."
>
> — Feynman, "O Americano, Outra Vez!" *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985).

**Context**: The students had memorized Brewster's law of polarization by reflection but had never connected it to the obvious fact that the light reflecting from the sea visible through their classroom window was polarized. The diagnostic gesture — make them apply the abstract claim to a concrete object in plain sight — is itself the test Feynman uses to surface fake-understanding.

### Q10 — Brazil: the self-propagating system

> "I couldn't see how anyone could be educated by this self-propagating system in which people pass exams, and teach others to pass exams, but nobody knows anything."
>
> — Feynman, "O Americano, Outra Vez!" *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985); final address to Brazilian Academy.

**Context**: Closing of his year-end speech to Brazilian education officials. The "self-propagating" framing is structural — Feynman is naming a systemic failure mode that produces credentialed cargo-cult without ever entering science.

### Q11 — Method of finding a new law (Messenger Lectures)

> "In general, we look for a new law by the following process. First, we guess it. Then, we compute the consequences of the guess, to see what, if this is right, if this law that we guessed is right, we see what it would imply. And then we compare those computation results to nature... If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. And that simple statement is the key to science. It doesn't make any difference how beautiful your guess is, it doesn't make a difference how smart you are... If it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong. That's all there is to it."
>
> — Feynman, "Seeking New Laws" (Messenger Lecture #7), Cornell 1964; *The Character of Physical Law*, ch. 7. <https://speakola.com/ideas/richard-feynman-seeking-new-laws-cornell-1964>

**Context**: The clearest statement Feynman ever gave of his epistemic procedure. The three-step structure (guess → derive consequence → compare to experiment) is — read together with Q7 — the same procedure his father drilled in him as a child, scaled up to physics.

### Q12 — Why-questions need a framework

> "When you explain a why, you have to be in some framework that you allow something to be true. Otherwise, you're perpetually asking why... You have to know what it is that you're permitted to understand and allow to be understood and known, and what it is you're not."
>
> — Feynman, BBC "Fun to Imagine: Magnets / Why Questions" (1983). <https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/W9rJv26sxs4g2B9bL/transcript-richard-feynman-on-why-questions>; <https://fs.blog/richard-feynman-on-why-questions/>

**Context**: Feynman's response when an interviewer asks "why" two magnets repel. He refuses to answer in familiar analogies (rubber bands) because the analogy would be false. The passage is the most explicit Feynman ever was about the *limits* of explanation-by-simplification — and is the natural counterweight to the "explain it simply" reading of his method.

### Q13 — Cannot reduce to a familiar analogy

> "I can't explain that attraction in terms of anything else that's familiar to you. For example, if we said the magnets attract like rubber bands, I would be cheating you. Because they're not connected by rubber bands."
>
> — Feynman, BBC "Fun to Imagine" (1983).

**Context**: Same interview, the operational corollary of Q12. Note: this is the same Feynman who in Q1 reduced all of physics to one sentence about atoms. The tension is load-bearing for the skill: he simplified relentlessly, but he refused to *falsify* by simplifying.

### Q14 — Aunt Minnie's hip: where causal regress should stop

> "For example, Aunt Minnie is in the hospital. Why? Because she went out, slipped on the ice, and broke her hip. That satisfies people. It satisfies, but it wouldn't satisfy someone who came from another planet and knew nothing about why when you break your hip you go to the hospital. How do you get to the hospital when the hip is broken? Well, because her husband, seeing that her hip was broken, called the hospital up and sent somebody to get her... Why did the husband call up the hospital? Because the husband is interested in his wife's welfare..."
>
> — Feynman, BBC "Fun to Imagine" (1983).

**Context**: Feynman is showing that "why" has no natural floor — every causal answer presupposes a layer that is itself a candidate for further why-questions. The point: explanation is always *relative to a frame of assumed knowledge*; first-principles thinking does not mean infinite regress but *making the frame explicit*.

### Q15 — Gleick's account of the Notebook of Things I Don't Know About

> "[He] opened a fresh notebook. On the title page he wrote: NOTEBOOK OF THINGS I DON'T KNOW ABOUT. For the first but not last time he reorganized his knowledge. He worked for weeks at disassembling each branch of physics, oiling the parts, and putting them back together, looking all the while for the raw edges and inconsistencies. He tried to find the essential kernels of each subject."
>
> — James Gleick, *Genius: The Life and Science of Richard Feynman* (Pantheon, 1992), ~p. 130; bibliography references Feynman's actual notebook "1940. Notebook of Things I Don't Know About. CIT." (Caltech archive). Cited via Cal Newport, "The Feynman Notebook Method," <https://calnewport.com/the-feynman-notebook-method/>

**Context**: This is the prototypical artifact later writers point to as "the Feynman technique." Feynman himself never named or systematized it; Gleick is reporting Feynman's pre-PhD-orals study practice (~1940, MIT/Princeton). Gleick is a biographer, not Feynman — so this is the strongest *evidence* of a method but not a *self-described* method. A reader-commenter on Newport's post notes Gleick also says the notebook "was not much use in preparing for the examination" — included here for honesty.

### Q16 — Goodstein: the freshman-lecture diagnostic

> Goodstein recounts asking Feynman: "Dick, explain to me, so that I can understand it, why spin one-half particles obey Fermi-Dirac statistics." Feynman said, "I'll prepare a freshman lecture on it." But he came back a few days later to say, "I couldn't do it. I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don't really understand it."
>
> — David L. & Judith R. Goodstein, *Feynman's Lost Lecture* (Norton, 1996), p. 52; first reported in Goodstein, "Richard P. Feynman, Teacher," *Physics Today* 42 (1989), pp. 70–75. <https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Talk:Richard_Feynman>

**Translation (ko)**: 굿스타인이 파인만에게 "스핀 1/2 입자가 페르미-디랙 통계를 따르는 이유를 1학년이 이해할 수 있게 설명해줘"라고 부탁했다. 파인만은 "1학년용 강의를 준비해보지" 라고 했지만, 며칠 뒤 돌아와 "안 되겠더라. 1학년 수준으로 못 줄이겠어. 그 말은 우리가 사실 그걸 정말로 이해하고 있지는 않다는 뜻이지" 라고 했다.

**Context**: This is the *verified primary source* for the popular paraphrase "if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it." Note carefully what Feynman actually said: "freshman level" (not "6-year-old," not "your grandmother"), and "*we* don't really understand it" — *we* as the physics community, not "you the listener." Feynman is using the simplification test diagnostically on himself and on his field, not as a universal pedagogical rule.

### Q17 — Disrespect for the experts / science is the belief in the ignorance of experts

> "Science is the belief in the ignorance of experts... When someone says, 'Science teaches such and such,' he is using the word incorrectly. Science doesn't teach anything; experience teaches it. If they say to you, 'Science has shown such and such,' you might ask, 'How does science show it? How did the scientists find out? How? What? Where?' It should not be 'science has shown' but 'this experiment, this effect, has shown.' And you have as much right as anyone else, upon hearing about the experiments — but be patient and listen to all the evidence — to judge whether a sensible conclusion has been arrived at."
>
> — Feynman, "What Is Science?" NSTA 1966 address; reprinted *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out*, p. 187. <http://feynman.com/science/what-is-science/>

**Context**: Feynman's first-principles stance applied to authority: *first principles* means demanding the chain from claim back to the actual observation, regardless of who is making the claim. Pairs naturally with Q11's "if it disagrees with experiment, it's wrong."

### Q18 — Take the world from another point of view

> "We think originally. We think of a new idea. We talk to each other, and we try to look at something from a new point of view... If you start at the beginning, which nobody wants to do — I mean, you come in to me now for an interview, and you ask me about the latest discoveries that are made. Nobody ever asks about a simple, ordinary phenomenon in the street."
>
> — Feynman, Yorkshire TV "Take the World from Another Point of View," 1973. <https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/35/2/PointofView.htm>

**Context**: Feynman complains that interviewers always want frontier results and never the boring foundational mechanisms that he considers actually more interesting. This is first-principles thinking as *aesthetic preference*: the foundations are where the live questions are.

### Q19 — Feynman's preface: "I don't think I did very well by the students"

> "My own point of view — which, however, does not seem to be shared by most of the people who worked with the students — is pessimistic. I don't think I did very well by the students. When I look at the way the majority of the students handled the problems on the examinations, I think that the system is a failure."
>
> — Feynman, Preface to *The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I* (June 1963). Quoted via <https://www.justinmath.com/according-to-feynman-himself-his-classes-were-a-failure-for-90-percent-of-his-students/>; cross-confirmed in physicists' forums.

**Translation (ko)**: "내 자신의 관점은 — 비록 학생들과 직접 작업한 대부분의 사람들은 공유하지 않는 관점이지만 — 비관적이다. 나는 학생들에게 잘 해주지 못했다고 생각한다. 시험에서 대다수의 학생이 문제를 다룬 방식을 보면, 이 시스템은 실패라고 생각한다."

**Context**: Feynman's *own self-criticism* of the very lectures that became canonical. This is the master figure himself acknowledging that the *Lectures*' approach — start from atoms, derive everything bottom-up — did not survive contact with an average student body. Foundational for the "limits / SKIP conditions" of a skill built on his method.

### Q20 — Preface: but those who got it, really got it

> "Of course, my friends point out to me that there were one or two dozen students who — very surprisingly — understood almost everything in all of the lectures, and who were quite active in working with the material and worrying about the many points in an excited and interested way. These people have now, I believe, a first-rate background in physics — and they are, after all, the ones I was trying to get at."
>
> — Feynman, Preface to *The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I* (1963).

**Context**: The other half of Q19. Feynman's method *did* work — for ~10% of students, the ones already pre-selected to think his way. This is decisive for any first-principles-style skill: the method is high-variance, not universally optimal.

### Q21 — Tips on Physics: learn by piddling around

> "In order to learn how to do that, you've got to forget the memorizing of formulas, and try to learn to understand the interrelationships of nature. That's very much more difficult at the beginning, but it's the only successful way... So I'd advise you — if you're not too far lost, completely befuddled and confused — that you forget about these lectures and piddle around by yourself. You'll learn infinitely better and easier and more completely by picking a problem for yourself that you find interesting to fiddle around with."
>
> — Feynman, "Prerequisites — Review Lecture A" in *Feynman's Tips on Physics* (lectures given 1961, published 2006), Ch. 1. <https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/TIPS_01.html>

**Context**: Feynman's direct advice to struggling Caltech sophomores. The recommendation is *not* "use the Feynman technique on the textbook" — it is "pick a concrete problem you find interesting and play with it yourself." Pairs critically with Q19/Q20: the method does not transfer through lecture.

## Critiques & limits (raw, 평가 금지)

### C1 — The simplification test fails on advanced topics whose complexity is the content

> "A critical limitation for expert-level topics is that simplifying a topic is required, but sometimes this oversimplification deletes the required complexity in advanced fields such as mathematics or theoretical physics... The technique's core strength — breaking down concepts into simple explanations — becomes a weakness for advanced, nuanced topics where the complexity itself is essential to true understanding."
>
> — Innovation Training et al., summary of Feynman-technique limitations. <https://www.innovationtraining.org/what-is-the-feynman-technique-and-how-to-use-it-for-learning/>; <https://noji.io/blog/the-feynman-technique/>

**Target**: The popular reading of Feynman's technique that says "any concept you can't explain to a child you don't understand."
**Context**: Practitioner-community articles surveying the technique's failure modes. Notably, this critique converges with Q12–Q13 (Feynman himself refusing to give a familiar-terms explanation of magnets) — i.e., Feynman would likely have agreed.

### C2 — Feynman's own diagnostic is about *us* not understanding, not about *you* failing to study harder

> Goodstein documents Feynman's actual phrasing: "I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we don't really understand it." (Not: "if *you* can't explain it, *you* don't understand it.") The commonly circulated paraphrase shifts the test from a community-level diagnostic about the state of physics to an individual-level shaming heuristic.
>
> — Goodstein, *Feynman's Lost Lecture*, p. 52, contrasted with the popular paraphrase tracked at <https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Talk:Richard_Feynman>

**Target**: The popular self-help reading of Feynman that treats inability-to-simplify as personal failure.
**Context**: Wikiquote talk page documents the drift between Feynman's actual statement and the version that now circulates in productivity culture.

### C3 — Feynman's own preface: the Lectures, as a method, did not work for most students

> "My own point of view... is pessimistic. I don't think I did very well by the students. When I look at the way the majority of the students handled the problems on the examinations, I think that the system is a failure."
>
> — Feynman, Preface to *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. I (1963). [Same source as Q19, repeated here as a critique-of-his-own-method.]

**Target**: The presumption that a first-principles-from-atoms presentation is universally superior pedagogy.
**Context**: Feynman is rendering his own verdict on the very method that later writers extract as "the Feynman approach." Self-assessment under depressed mood (per Sands' account), but Feynman never publicly retracted this assessment in print.

### C4 — Physics Forums: Feynman's lectures are widely judged not pedagogically usable as a first text

> Demystifier: Feynman is "much deeper" and "suitable only to physics majors," in contrast to Halliday & Resnick which is "designed to make physics as simple as possible." Vela: Feynman's course "was targeting" a specific student type, and "the vast majority of students, even those majoring in physics, do not fall in that category." Vanhees71 recommends studying "a more conventional treatment besides the Feynman books."
>
> — Physics Forums thread, "Why are the Feynman lectures not good for beginners?" <https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/why-are-the-feynman-lectures-not-good-for-beginners.916301/>

**Target**: The claim that Feynman-style derivations are the natural starting point for learners.
**Context**: A working physicists' community consensus that Feynman's text is best used as supplement *after* a conventional text — i.e., first-principles presentation is not first-principles pedagogy.

### C5 — Feynman's own limit: there is no familiar-terms explanation of magnets

> "I really can't do a good job, any job, of explaining magnetic force in terms of something else that you're more familiar with, because I don't understand it in terms of anything else that you're more familiar with."
>
> — Feynman, BBC "Fun to Imagine" (1983). [Same source as Q13.]
>
> Peter Coles' commentary: "I feel it starts off very uncomfortably, with considerable tension between interviewer and interviewee... He may come across as a bit arrogant — I don't know — but there's an integrity about his reply, his refusal to 'cheat'."
> — Peter Coles, "The Feynman Reaction" (2010), <https://telescoper.blog/2010/11/05/the-feynman-reaction/>

**Target**: Naïve readings of Feynman that take "explain it to a child" as universally achievable.
**Context**: Feynman explicitly refusing the simplification move he is later credited with elevating into a method. A working astrophysicist (Coles) notes the tension audiences feel with this refusal — important because it shows Feynman's first-principles stance can read as obstructive in conversation.

### C6 — Illusion of explanatory depth: simplification can manufacture false confidence

> "The Feynman Technique effectively reveals knowledge gaps, [but] it must be paired with rigorous thinking to avoid merely creating oversimplified explanations rather than achieving true understanding... The human brain prefers simplicity over complexity, often reducing intricate issues to oversimplified explanations that provide a sense of clarity but distort reality."
>
> — Summary of illusion-of-explanatory-depth literature and the Feynman technique, <https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/the-illusion-of-explanatory-depth>; <https://dev.to/kanywst/the-feynman-technique-2026-a-cognitive-algorithm-to-kill-the-illusion-of-competence-57c1>

**Target**: The assumption that producing a smooth simple explanation = actually understanding.
**Context**: Cognitive-science framing: a fluent simplification can paradoxically deepen the illusion of understanding (the very bias the technique is supposed to defeat) if the test is "does my explanation feel coherent" rather than "does it predict what the system does in a case I haven't seen."

### C7 — Gleick: Feynman's own notebook "was not much use" for the actual exam

> A commenter on Cal Newport's article reports that James Gleick's *Genius* notes Feynman's "Notebook of Things I Don't Know About" "was not much use in preparing for the examination."
>
> — Cal Newport, "The Feynman Notebook Method," comments section, <https://calnewport.com/the-feynman-notebook-method/>

**Target**: The straightforward inference "the notebook practice is what made Feynman Feynman."
**Context**: Gleick's biography contains this caveat alongside the celebrated description (Q15). The notebook reorganized Feynman's *own* understanding — which is what Feynman valued — but it was not the proximate cause of his exam performance and is not advertised by Gleick as a transferable study tip.

## Recurring observations (raw, 해석 금지)

- "Knowing the name of X" vs. "knowing X" appears in at least three independent sources spanning 1966 → 1981 → 1973 (Q5, Q6, Q18), each attributed to his father. The phrasing is near-identical across decades.
- The phrase "first principle" appears in Feynman's own voice only once in this corpus (Q2, Cargo Cult Science) — and Feynman uses it to mean *the prerequisite epistemic posture* ("you must not fool yourself"), not "starting from axioms." The "first-principles thinking" label as applied to Feynman is largely a retrospective frame.
- The three-step structure (guess → derive consequence → compare to reality) recurs as a procedure in Q7 (father / bird pecking), Q11 (Seeking New Laws), and Q9 (Brazil polaroid test). Same shape, three different scales.
- Feynman pairs *aggressive simplification* (Q1: all of physics in one sentence) with *aggressive refusal to simplify* (Q12–Q13: cannot explain magnets in familiar terms). The two appear together in his work; the popular "Feynman technique" reads only the first half.
- Self-criticism of his teaching is present in Feynman's own voice (Q19, Q21) — not just in third-party critique. Critique sources (C3, C4) build on Feynman's own preface.
- Feynman repeatedly anchors his method in his *father*, not in physics: Q5, Q6, Q7, Q18. The acquired-from-childhood framing is consistent.
- Brazil chapter (Q8–Q10) and Cargo Cult Science (Q3–Q4) describe the *failure mode* the method is built to defeat — fluent surface that fails on a concrete prediction. Two independent set-pieces, same diagnosis.
- The "Feynman technique" 4-step recipe (pick concept → explain simply → find gaps → simplify) circulating in Korean and English self-help (e.g. Brunch source) does not appear in Feynman's own writing in this corpus. It is a post-hoc distillation.
