## Quotes & evidence

### Q1 — Definition of being mode
> "By being I refer to the mode of existence in which one neither has anything nor craves to have something, but is joyous, employs one's faculties productively, is oned to the world."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?* (1976), p. 28 [page per Source #6]; see also Source #2 (There Seem to Be Other Ways) and Source #9 (Juodis Medium commentary).

**Context**: From Chapter 1, where Fromm first defines the two modes in distinction from each other before applying them to daily activities in Chapter 2.

### Q2 — The two modes as fundamental experience
> "Having and being are two fundamental modes of experience, the respective strengths of which determine the difference between the characters of individuals and various types of social character."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 1; quoted in Source #8 (There Seem to Be Other Ways).

**Context**: Statement of the thesis — the modes are not merely behaviors but determine character structure at both the individual and the social level.

### Q3 — Language as the symptom (the activity / having confusion)
> "To express an activity by 'to have' in connection with a noun is an erroneous use of language, because processes and activities cannot be possessed; they can only be experienced."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 1; quoted in Source #8.

**Context**: Fromm's linguistic diagnosis: when we say "I have a problem" instead of "I am troubled," or "I have insomnia" instead of "I cannot sleep," we have shifted experience into property. He uses this to argue the having mode infiltrates how we describe inner states.

### Q4 — Learning in the having mode (the lecture-hall student)
> "Students in the having mode must have but one aim: to hold onto what they have 'learned', either by entrusting it firmly to their memories or by carefully guarding their notes."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?* (1979 Abacus ed., London), p. 38 [page per Source #6 (1976 Harper & Row ed.); London Abacus citation per Source #5 (infed.org)].

**Context**: Chapter 2, opening illustration of "Learning." Fromm pictures students who listen to a lecture, hear the logical structure of the words, and "write down every word in their loose-leaf notebooks—so that, later on, they can memorize their notes and thus pass an examination." They become "owners of a collection of statements made by somebody else."

### Q5 — Learning in the being mode (the active student)
> "The process of learning has an entirely different quality for students in the being mode… Instead of being passive receptacles of words and ideas, they listen, they *hear*, and most important, they *receive* and they *respond* in an active, productive way."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 2 ("Learning"); quoted in Source #5 (infed.org).

**Context**: Direct counter-image to Q4. Students in the being mode come to a lecture already with questions, are changed by what they hear, and produce new thinking rather than store received content.

### Q6 — Identity collapse in the having mode
> "If I am what I have and if what I have is lost, who then am I? Nobody but a defeated, deflated, pathetic testimony to a wrong way of living."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?* (1976), Chapter 4; quoted in Source #9 (Juodis Medium) and Source #6 (JR Cutler underlinings).

**Context**: The core existential consequence of identifying with possessions: loss of property becomes loss of self.

### Q7 — Security and identity in the being mode
> "The anxiety and insecurity engendered by the danger of losing what one has are absent in the being mode. If I am who I am and not what I have, nobody can deprive me of or threaten my security and my sense of identity. My center is within myself; my capacity for being and for expressing my essential powers is part of my character structure and depends on me."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*; quoted in Source #2 (Daily Philosophy / libquotes attribution chain) and confirmed in Source #6.

**Context**: The structural counterpart of Q6. Once identity is anchored in being, possessions can come and go without altering the self.

### Q8 — Knowing as activity (not stored content)
> "Knowing means to penetrate through the surface, in order to arrive at the roots, and hence the causes."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?* (1976), p. 47 [page per Source #6 (JR Cutler underlinings)].

**Context**: Chapter 2 ("Knowing") — knowledge in the being mode is a process of going beneath appearances, not the accumulation of facts to be retrieved.

### Q9 — Eckhart on being empty of one's knowledge (Fromm's appropriation)
> "When [Eckhart] says that 'a man ought to be empty of his own knowledge,' he does not mean that one should forget what one knows, but rather one should forget that one knows—we should not look at our knowledge as a possession, in which we find security and which gives us a sense of identity."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 4 (on Eckhart's having vs being); quoted in Source #2 (web search excerpt) and consistent with Fromm's Eckhart chapter in the book.

**Context**: Fromm uses Meister Eckhart's sermons as the medieval / mystical anchor for the being mode. The distinction is not between knowing and not-knowing but between holding knowledge as property versus moving through knowledge as an act.

### Q10 — Authority as a relation, not a possession
> "Authority is not a quality one person 'has,' in the sense that he has property or physical qualities. Authority refers to an interpersonal relation in which one person looks upon another as somebody superior to him."
>
> — Erich Fromm, attributed (Source #6 cites *To Have or To Be?*, Ch. 2, pp. 45–47 on "having vs being authority"). Source: https://quotefancy.com/quote/803149/

**Context**: Chapter 2's "exercising authority" section. Fromm distinguishes the "having of authority" (a title, a rank, a position) from "being an authority" — which rests on competence and integrity and disappears the moment those qualities erode.

### Q11 — Love as activity, not possession
> "Love is an active power in a man… Love is the active concern for the life and the growth of that which we love."
>
> — *The Art of Loving* (1956), pp. 16 & 21 [pages per Source: Wikipedia summary citing the Harper editions].

**Context**: This 1956 formulation is the seedbed of the being-mode treatment of love in *To Have or To Be?* twenty years later. In *To Have or To Be?* (Chapter 2, "Loving") Fromm makes the same point: love is not a thing one "has" or "falls into" but an activity one performs.

### Q12 — "Standing in love" vs "falling for"
> "Love is an activity, not a passive affect; it is a 'standing in,' not a 'falling for.'"
>
> — *The Art of Loving* (1956); also reformulated in *To Have or To Be?* (Chapter 2).

**Context**: The grammatical move ("standing" rather than "falling") is Fromm's signature illustration that love belongs to the being mode and that the English idiom of falling already presupposes a having-mode metaphysics (something that happens to you, that you then possess).

### Q13 — The two-poems contrast (Tennyson vs Basho)
> Tennyson: "Flower in the crannied wall, I pluck you out of the crannies, / I hold you here, root and all, in my hand…"
> Basho: "When I look carefully / I see the nazuna blooming / By the hedge!"
>
> — *To Have or To Be?* (1976), Chapter 1 ("The Difference Between Having and Being in Two Poems"); Fromm cites D. T. Suzuki's commentary on the Basho haiku. Texts of both poems verified in Source #2 (search hits) and Source #8 (Searcher-Seeker blog).

**Context**: Fromm's opening illustration of the whole framework. Tennyson must possess the flower to know it (and kills it in plucking it); Basho merely sees and lets it be. The example fixes the criterion for diagnosing any activity afterward: does the relation require taking, or does it leave the object intact while changing the subject?

### Q14 — "Existential having" (the legitimate having)
> "Human existence requires that we have, keep, take care of, and use certain things in order to survive… This form of having may be called existential having."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 1; quoted in Source #9 (Juodis Medium).

**Context**: Important caveat. Fromm is not telling people to own nothing. He distinguishes "existential having" (the food I need, the tools I use) from "characterological having" (the orientation that makes possessing the meaning of life). Only the latter is the target of the critique.

### Q15 — The optimum-knowledge axis (having more vs knowing deeper)
> "The optimum knowledge in the being mode is to know more deeply, while in the having mode it is to have more knowledge."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*, Chapter 2 ("Knowing"); paraphrased from Fromm in Source's search-result excerpt; consistent with Q8.

**Context**: Two different success criteria for knowledge-work: depth (vertical, transformative) vs. quantity (horizontal, accumulative). The same student-hours can produce either, depending on the underlying mode.

### Q16 — Marxian source: the inversion of life under capital
> "The less you are, the less you express your life, the more you have, the greater is your alienated life and the greater is the saving of your alienated being."
>
> — Karl Marx, *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, Third Manuscript ("Human Needs and the Division of Labour Under the Rule of Private Property"). Quoted in multiple Marxist archive sources; Fromm cites and develops this passage in *Marx's Concept of Man* (1961, Ch. 5) and again in *To Have or To Be?* (Chapter 5–6 on Marx).

**Context**: This is the genealogical root of Fromm's having/being inversion. The capitalist denial of life (saving, abstaining, accumulating) trades being for having. Fromm reads this Marx passage as the diagnostic statement that the modern character structure has solved the wrong optimization problem.

### Q17 — "I am what I have" — the property motto
> "I am what I have."
>
> — *The Art of Being* (1989, posthumous); quoted in Source #7 (The Marginalian).

**Context**: In *The Art of Being* Fromm contrasts three mottoes: "I am what I have" (property mode), "I am what I do" (activity mode), "I am what I am" (being mode). Useful as a one-line diagnostic axis applicable to any decision.

### Q18 — Goal statement
> "Our goal should be to be much, not to have much."
>
> — *To Have or To Be?*; quoted in Source #2 (libquotes summary of the book).

**Context**: The maxim Fromm closes the framework around.

## Recurring observations (raw)
- The contrast is consistently structured as: same activity (learning / reading / loving / remembering / exercising authority) is diagnosed in two modes by *what the subject does with the object* — store/possess vs. engage/transform.
- The activity-vs-thing distinction is grammatical for Fromm: nouns invite the having mode, verbs the being mode. He keeps pointing at idioms like "I have a problem," "to fall in love," "to have authority," "to hold an opinion."
- Anxiety and identity collapse appear as a paired symptom of the having mode (Q6, Q7); joy and "being oned to the world" as the paired symptom of the being mode (Q1).
- The being mode is consistently described with verbs of opening, penetrating, responding, receiving, growing, employing — not verbs of holding or storing.
- Across Q4–Q5, Q8, Q10, Q11, Q12, Q15 the same diagnostic shape recurs: name the activity, then ask whether the criterion of success is *accumulation/retention* or *transformation/depth*.
- Eckhart and Marx are Fromm's two doctrinal anchors — one mystical, one materialist — both arguing (in Fromm's reading) that *having more correlates with being less*.
- The framework is explicitly designed to be applied across the whole life surface: Chapter 2 alone covers learning, remembering, conversing, reading, exercising authority, knowing, faith, loving. Fromm treats this catalogue as illustrative, not exhaustive.
