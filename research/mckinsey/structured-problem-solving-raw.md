## Quotes & evidence

### Q1 — MECE: definition by its inventor (Minto)
> "The concept required the groups of ideas to be MECE — divided pieces that were mutually exclusive of each other and collectively exhaustive in terms of the whole."
>
> — Reported as Barbara Minto's own framing, McKinsey alumni profile "Barbara Minto: 'MECE: I invented it, so I get to say how to pronounce it'", McKinsey & Company (alumni site). Same wording is preserved in the *Wikipedia: MECE principle* primary citation back to Minto.

**Context**: Minto's recollection of how she arrived at the rule while editing reports at McKinsey's Cleveland office in the late 1960s; she noticed she was always reshaping ideas into a pyramid and that the groups underneath any node had to satisfy this property.

### Q2 — MECE pronunciation, ownership of the term
> "I invented it, so I get to say how to pronounce it."
>
> — Barbara Minto, McKinsey Alumni profile (URL above). She pronounces it "mee-see" (rhymes with "niece").

**Context**: Headline quote of the McKinsey-published Minto profile, settling the long-running pronunciation debate among consultants.

### Q3 — Three rules of grouping in a pyramid (Minto)
> "Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them, (2) ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea, (3) ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered."
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, opening chapter (3rd ed., 2009). Reproduced verbatim in Will Larson's reading notes (lethain.com/pyramid-principle/).

**Context**: Minto's core structural rule for any pyramid of ideas — and by extension any document or argument that should reflect clear thinking.

### Q4 — Answer-first / top-down sequencing (Minto)
> "Controlling the sequence in which you present your ideas is the single most important act necessary to clear writing. The clearest sequence is always to give the summarizing idea before you give the individual ideas being summarized."
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Part I "Logic in Writing", opening chapters. Reproduced verbatim in lethain.com/pyramid-principle/.

**Context**: This is the foundational claim behind "answer first": present the top of the pyramid before any of the supporting ideas. Minto's *because* is cognitive — the reader can hold the summary and use it to organize the details that follow.

### Q5 — Why pyramid order works (Minto, reader-cognition rationale)
> "The mind automatically sorts information into distinctive pyramidal groupings in order to comprehend it."
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, opening chapter. Reproduced in StrategyU summary "The Pyramid Principle: Book Summary & Review (Part 1)".

> "If a person's writing is unclear, it is most likely because the ordering of the ideas conflicts with the capability of the reader's mind to process them."
>
> — Same source.

> "You must recognize that a reader, no matter how intelligent he is, has only a limited amount of mental energy available to him."
>
> — Same source.

**Context**: Minto grounds the pyramid not in stylistic preference but in cognitive load — the reader's mind is the bottleneck, so order must match how minds aggregate ideas.

### Q6 — The four possible orderings inside a pyramid (Minto)
> "There are only four possible logical ways in which to order a set of ideas: deductively, chronologically, structurally, comparatively."
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*. Reproduced verbatim in lethain.com/pyramid-principle/.

**Context**: After enforcing MECE at a grouping, Minto demands an explicit logical order; without one the "same kind of idea" rule isn't really binding.

### Q7 — SCQA introduction structure (Minto)
> "It should begin by establishing for the reader the time and the place of a Situation. In that Situation something will have occurred (known as the Complication) that caused him to raise (or would cause him to raise) the Question to which your document will give him the Answer."
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, Chapter on the introduction. Reproduced verbatim in lethain.com/pyramid-principle/.

> "[The introduction] permits you to make sure you and the reader are standing in the same place before you take him by the hand."
>
> — Barbara Minto, same chapter. Reproduced in strategyu.co.

**Context**: The four-part introduction (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer) is the on-ramp into the pyramid. The Answer at the bottom of the introduction is the top of the pyramid.

### Q8 — "No 'Background' sections" (Minto, anti-pattern)
> "As a rule of thumb, you never want to have a section labeled 'Background' or 'Introduction.'"
>
> — Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*. Reproduced in strategyu.co.

**Context**: Minto's reasoning (paraphrased in the same passage): a "Background" section sits at the wrong level of abstraction and lists subjects rather than ideas, so the implicit argument never gets made.

### Q9 — McKinsey problem-solving in three words (Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way*)
> "Structure, structure, structure. MECE, MECE, MECE. Hypothesis-driven, hypothesis-driven, hypothesis-driven."
>
> — Quoted by Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (McGraw-Hill, 1999), as words of "a former associate in the Düsseldorf and San Francisco offices." Reproduced in MConsultingPrep's summary of the book.

**Context**: Rasiel uses this as the alumnus-distilled motto of McKinsey's problem-solving DNA — three legs of the same stool.

### Q10 — MECE as the test for an issue list (Rasiel)
> "Is each one a separate and distinct issue? If so, then your issue list is mutually exclusive."
>
> "Does every aspect of the problem come under one (and only one) of these issues — that is, have you thought of everything? If so, then your issues are collectively exhaustive."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (1999), chapter on MECE. Reproduced as paired test in the Goodreads Rasiel quote page.

**Context**: Rasiel turns MECE from a definition into an operational check ("ask these two questions of your issue list").

### Q11 — Issue tree cardinality rule (Rasiel)
> "A good McKinsey issue list contains neither fewer than two nor more than five top-line issues (of course, three is best)."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (1999). Goodreads Rasiel author-quotes page.

**Context**: A practical constraint on issue-tree branching that mirrors Miller's "magical number seven" rationale that Minto cites.

### Q12 — Hypothesis-driven approach: figure out the answer first
> "Figure out the solution to the problem before you start [solving it]."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (1999), chapter on the initial hypothesis. Goodreads author-quotes.

**Context**: The defining mental move of hypothesis-driven problem solving: state a guess up front, then orient analysis toward proving or killing it.

### Q13 — Don't boil the ocean (Rasiel, anti-pattern + his own ongoing use of the phrase)
> "[At McKinsey,] we don't boil the ocean."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (1999), as quoted back by interviewer Will Bachman: "It really makes the book come alive, and illustrates it, and kind of gloms onto it." Source: Umbrex *Unleashed* podcast Episode 101 transcript, p.4. Rasiel himself reuses the metaphor on p.7 of the same transcript:

> "It's really hard to boil the ocean, and the ocean's gotten a lot bigger."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *Unleashed* podcast Ep. 101 (2019), p.7.

**Context**: The injunction is against exhaustive, undirected analysis. Conn's "rigorous prioritization" (Q19) is the positive form of the same rule.

### Q14 — McKinsey's structured method as a frame for *any* "what should I do?" (Conn)
> "For me, problem solving is the answer to the question 'What should I do?' It's interesting when there's uncertainty and complexity, and when it's meaningful because there are consequences."
>
> — Charles Conn, *McKinsey Podcast: How to master the seven-step problem-solving process*, September 2019, p.2.

**Context**: Conn frames structured problem solving as a domain-agnostic procedure for any decision under uncertainty — climate policy, where to live, business strategy — anything that resolves to "what should I do?"

### Q15 — The 7-step process and *why* it's 7 steps (Conn)
> "I'm a big fan of structured problem solving. By following steps, we can more clearly understand what problem it is we're solving, what are the components of the problem that we're solving, which components are the most important ones for us to pay attention to, which analytic techniques we should apply to those, and how we can synthesize what we've learned back into a compelling story. That's all it is, at its heart."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.2.

> "I think sometimes when people think about seven steps, they assume that there's a rigidity to this. That's not it at all. It's actually to give you the scope for creativity, which often doesn't exist when your problem solving is muddled."
>
> — Charles Conn, same source, p.2.

**Context**: Conn's defense against the obvious objection ("isn't this just a checklist?"). The point of the seven steps is the opposite: they protect creative bandwidth by absorbing structure work, so the team has space to think.

### Q16 — Step 1: Defining the problem (Sarrazin)
> "It is surprising how often people jump past this step and make a bunch of assumptions. The most powerful thing is to step back and ask the basic questions — 'What are we trying to solve? What are the constraints that exist? What are the dependencies?' Let's make those explicit and really push the thinking and defining. At McKinsey, we spend an enormous amount of time in writing that little statement, and the statement, if you're a logic purist, is great. You debate, 'Is it an "or"? Is it an "and"? What's the action verb?' Because all these specific words help you get to the heart of what matters."
>
> — Hugo Sarrazin, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.3.

**Context**: This is the working procedure for the McKinsey problem statement: a written sentence, debated word-by-word ("or" vs "and", action verb), refined over hours of the first team meeting.

### Q17 — Why bad problem definition is catastrophic (Sarrazin)
> "When you spend an enormous amount of time, in the first meeting of the different stakeholders, debating this and having different people put forward what they think the problem definition is, you realize that people have completely different views of why they're here. That, to me, is the most important step."
>
> — Hugo Sarrazin, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.3.

> "How often do especially bright young people run off with half of the idea about what the problem is and start collecting data and start building models — only to discover that they've really gone off half-cocked." / "And in the wrong direction."
>
> — Sarrazin and Conn, exchange, same source p.3.

**Context**: The "fast-start" failure mode: smart people skip definition, race to data, and produce confidently-built answers to the wrong question.

### Q18 — Step 2: Disaggregate with logic trees (Conn)
> "My favorite step is step two, which is to use logic trees to disaggregate the problem. Every problem we're solving has some complexity and some uncertainty in it. The only way that we can really get our team working on the problem is to take the problem apart into logical pieces."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.3.

> "What we find, of course, is that the way to disaggregate the problem often gives you an insight into the answer to the problem quite quickly. I love to do two or three different cuts at it, each one giving a bit of a different insight into what might be going wrong. By doing sensible disaggregations, using logic trees, we can figure out which parts of the problem we should be looking at, and we can assign those different parts to team members."
>
> — Charles Conn, same source, p.3.

**Context**: Conn's key move — *try multiple disaggregations* of the same problem. The act of cutting it different ways is itself a generator of hypotheses, not just an organizational exercise.

### Q19 — Step 3: Prioritize / prune the tree (Conn)
> "The third step, which we also emphasize, along with good problem definition, is rigorous prioritization — we ask the questions 'How important is this lever or this branch of the tree in the overall outcome that we seek to achieve? How much can I move that lever?' Obviously, we try and focus our efforts on ones that have a big impact on the problem and the ones that we have the ability to change."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.4.

> "People spend a lot of time arguing about branches that are either not important or that none of us can change."
>
> — Charles Conn, same source, p.4.

**Context**: The pruning criterion is two-dimensional: (a) impact on the outcome × (b) our ability to move it. Branches that fail either test are dropped — not analyzed further. With the Pacific salmon project he led for the Moore Foundation, ocean conditions were "a big lever, but not one that we could adjust," so the team focused on habitats and harvesting.

### Q20 — Step 4: Work plan with day-one / one-hour answers (Sarrazin & Conn)
> "There's always a level-one answer, there's a level-two answer, there's a level-three answer. Without being too flippant, I can solve any problem during a good dinner with wine. It won't have a whole lot of backing."
>
> — Hugo Sarrazin, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.4.

> "We encourage our teams everywhere to do that. We call it the one-day answer or the one-hour answer. In work planning, we're always iterating. Every time you see a 50-page work plan that stretches out to three months, you know it's wrong. It will be outmoded very quickly by that learning process that you described."
>
> — Charles Conn, same source, p.4.

**Context**: This is the operational form of hypothesis-driven thinking inside the 7-step process: you must always be able to state a *current* best answer, even if its support is thin. The plan is to upgrade that answer's evidence — not to defer the answer until evidence is complete.

### Q21 — Step 5: Analyze — start with simple heuristics (Conn)
> "For me, the most important thing is that we start with simple heuristics and explanatory statistics before we go off and use the big-gun tools. We need to understand the shape and scope of our problem before we start applying these massive and complex analytical approaches."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.5.

> "Just as 20 years ago, when we were doing statistical analysis, we knew that we needed good model definition, we still need a good understanding of our algorithms and really good problem definition before we launch off into big data sets and unknown algorithms."
>
> — Charles Conn, same source, p.5.

**Context**: Even with ML available, Conn keeps the order: define → disaggregate → prioritize → simple statistics → then heavy tools. The structure is upstream of the math.

### Q22 — Step 6+7: Synthesize and Communicate (Conn)
> "I take six and seven together, and this is the place where young problem solvers often make a mistake. They've got their analysis, and they assume that's the answer, and of course it isn't the answer. The ability to synthesize the pieces that came out of the analysis and begin to weave those into a story that helps people answer the question 'What should I do?' This is back to where we started. If we can't synthesize, and we can't tell a story, then our decision maker can't find the answer to 'What should I do?'"
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.6.

**Context**: The output of analysis is *not* the deliverable. The deliverable is a story that lets a decision-maker move. This is the explicit handoff from the 7-step process into Minto-style pyramid communication.

### Q23 — Express uncertainty in the recommendation (Conn)
> "Often, people lack humility in the way they make their recommendations: 'This is the answer.' They're very precise, and I think we would all be well-served to say, 'This is a likely answer under the following sets of conditions' and then make the level of uncertainty clearer, if that is appropriate. It doesn't mean you're always in the gray zone; it doesn't mean you don't have a point of view. It just means that you can be explicit about the certainty of your answer when you make that recommendation."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.7.

**Context**: A modification of "answer first" — yes, lead with the answer, but qualify the conditions. The answer is a function of premises; surface the premises.

### Q24 — Common mistakes (Conn & McLean, Prichard interview)
> "The most common mistakes in complex problem solving are weak problem statements, poor problem disaggregation, and insufficient attention to good team norms."
>
> "When teams fail to disaggregate problems well — we use logic trees and usually try several versions — they often fall prey to asserting answers from past models."
>
> — Charles Conn & Rob McLean, Skip Prichard "7 Steps to Problem Solving" interview (2019).

**Context**: The three named failure modes — and the explicit naming of "asserting answers from past models" as the cost of skipping step 2. (These appear as direct authorial language in Prichard's published transcript, though Prichard does not always attribute line-by-line.)

### Q25 — "Anchor the team in 'what problem are we solving?'" (Conn, same interview)
> "Teams often race off to data-gathering without really understanding what problem they are trying to solve."
>
> — Charles Conn & Rob McLean, Skip Prichard interview (2019).

**Context**: The same anti-pattern Sarrazin describes in Q17, restated by the authors of *Bulletproof Problem Solving* as the modal failure they see.

### Q26 — How McKinsey starts: facts (Rasiel)
> "Problem solving at the Firm begins with facts. Having drawn up an initial hypothesis for the problem, the team then races to gather the facts necessary (when put through the appropriate analyses) to support or refute it."
>
> — Ethan Rasiel, *The McKinsey Way* (1999), reproduced/paraphrased in MConsultingPrep's chapter-by-chapter summary of the book.

**Context**: The full loop in one sentence: hypothesis → facts → analysis → support or refute. Note the order: hypothesis comes *before* facts, not after.

### Q27 — Conn on synthesis and the public-square failure mode
> "We end up in exactly the same place where we started, which is people speaking across each other, past each other in the public square, rather than actually working together, shoulder to shoulder, to crack these important problems."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.6.

**Context**: Why synthesis matters beyond consulting: without a shared structured method, disagreement is not productive — it's parallel monologues.

### Q28 — Iteration is part of the design (Conn)
> "I love the way you've described that, because, again, some people think of problem solving as a linear thing, but of course what's critical is that it's iterative. As you say, you can solve the problem in one day or even one hour. … In work planning, we're always iterating."
>
> — Charles Conn, McKinsey Podcast (Sept 2019), p.4.

**Context**: The seven steps are not a waterfall. Each pass through the loop tightens the problem statement, refines the tree, and updates the day-one answer.

## Recurring observations (raw, no interpretation)

- The word **"structure"** appears in every primary source as a load-bearing term, often tripled ("structure, structure, structure" — Rasiel; "structured problem solving" — Conn x 5; "structured, fact-based, and hypothesis-driven" — Rasiel).
- The **same three-word triad** appears in Rasiel: *structure / MECE / hypothesis-driven*.
- **"Answer first"** appears in multiple forms: Minto's "summarizing idea before … individual ideas being summarized" (Q4); Conn's "one-day answer / one-hour answer" (Q20); Rasiel's "figure out the solution to the problem before you start" (Q12). All three frame the answer not as the *end* of analysis but as its *organizing hypothesis*.
- **Anti-pattern phrases** that recur: "boil the ocean" (Rasiel, twice; quoted back as canon in 2019 interview), "race off to data-gathering" (Conn), "jump past this step" (Sarrazin), "gone off half-cocked / in the wrong direction" (Conn/Sarrazin), "asserting answers from past models" (Conn).
- **Cognitive grounding** appears in Minto (limited reader energy, mind sorts into pyramids) and Conn (availability bias, "sunflower bias" / hierarchy bias — Q from p.5 of podcast where Conn names "Availability bias is the one that I'm always alert to" and "sunflower bias" — included implicitly in the structure rationale).
- **Tree imagery** appears in every author: Minto's *pyramid*, Rasiel's *issue tree*, Conn's *logic tree*. All three insist the children at each node must MECE-cover the parent.
- **The synthesis step is repeatedly named as the most common failure point** — both Conn ("young problem solvers often make a mistake … assume the analysis is the answer", Q22) and Minto (the "Background section" anti-pattern, Q8) name the same shape of error: stopping at the bottom of the pyramid instead of climbing back to the top.
- **Iteration over linearity**: Conn ("not a linear thing … iterative … one-day answer / one-hour answer"), Sarrazin ("test and learn, test and learn"), Rasiel ("races to gather the facts … to support or refute it").
- **Numerical heuristics for breadth**: Minto's pyramid groupings (around the "magical number seven, plus or minus two" of Miller); Rasiel's "neither fewer than two nor more than five top-line issues (of course, three is best)."
- **Problem statement is treated as a written artifact, debated word-by-word** (Sarrazin Q16). Conn names the same idea as the "problem definition canvas" / problem definition step 1 in *Bulletproof Problem Solving*.

## Gaps / open questions

- **Marvin Bower** — primary-source quotes from Bower specifically on problem solving (rather than on firm values / professionalism) were not located within the time budget. His HBR-era essays and *Perspective on McKinsey* (1979) would be the right place to look.
- **Bulletproof Problem Solving — "seven characteristics of a good problem statement"** — I did not get a verbatim from the book itself. Multiple secondary summaries list the seven properties (outcome-focused, specific and measurable, time-bound, addresses decision-maker values/boundaries, allows scope for creativity, solved at the highest possible level, etc.) but I avoided quoting them as Conn/McLean's words. The book itself (Chapter 2) is the right primary source.
- **TOSCA framework** — I expected this would appear in *Bulletproof Problem Solving* but it is actually from Garrette/Phelps/Sibony, *Cracked It!*. So TOSCA is **not** McKinsey-primary; Conn/McLean's "problem definition canvas" is the McKinsey equivalent and was not captured verbatim here.
- **Minto's book — direct paginated citations** — I have verbatim Minto quotes (Q3–Q8) reproduced in trustworthy secondary write-ups (lethain.com, strategyu.co) but I do not have page numbers from the printed book. A pass against the physical 3rd edition would let the summarizer pin these.
- **McKinsey Quarterly long-form on structured problem solving** — there are essays beyond the September 2019 podcast; I did not survey them.
- **Charles Conn TED-style talk** — referenced in search results but transcript not captured.
- **The "obligation to dissent"** appears in the Prichard interview as a named McKinsey norm; the primary source (likely Bower or the McKinsey values document) was not located.
- **Did not include**: undated "Marvin Bower problem-solving" quotes circulating on quote-aggregator sites — could not verify primary attribution.
